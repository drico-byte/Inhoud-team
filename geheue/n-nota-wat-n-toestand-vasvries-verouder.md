**Gr 6 Social Sciences, 29 September 2026.** Seven specs told writers the shared-wordings
list held "three" or "four" settled terms — one said it was deliberately empty — and named
as *still open* terms that were already decided. Two writers reported it independently. A
real fault: a writer reading it would write a second wording for a settled term.

**So I prepended a dated correction to all eight specs. It said the list now holds 30
settled wordings, and it named the two terms that had been withdrawn as false.**

Within the hour I settled both of those terms and the count reached 40. My own correction
was then telling writers to replace wordings that were by that point correct, and naming
as withdrawn two terms that were settled. A writer caught it and reported the injected
list and my note contradicting each other.

**The rule: a note that freezes a state goes stale exactly as fast as the one it replaces.**
I fixed a stale-count note by writing a stale-count note. The replacement now names **no
count and no term** — it says only that the injected list is the sole authority, that a term
present in it is mandatory and word for word, and that a term absent from it is not yet
settled. That sentence stays true through every future change.

**How to apply.** When correcting a field that went stale because it recorded a *state*,
never write the current state into the correction. Point at whatever holds the state live —
the injected list, the file, the script's output — and describe the *rule* for reading it.
If I catch myself typing a number, a count, or a list of names into a note that will be read
later, that is the moment to stop: those are the things that rot. A rule does not.

**The same shape, from the same day:** the runner's own profiler line was right to say a
config's *name* is not a budget's provenance; and the spec fields that said "the wording
will be reconciled after drafting" were fine as a rule and wrong as a state once the
reconciliation had happened.

becomes the stale thing. And [[n-verslag-in-die-logboom-kan-verouderd-wees]], which is the
same failure in a report rather than a note.

## An order that asks for a delivery must retire itself in its own field

1 October 2026, and I managed both halves of this in one day. I added a requirement
saying a lesson's title must not claim everything in it is a child's entitlement. Into
the same field I put two things that could not survive being acted on:

1. **The current state, quoted.** *"Die huidige titel, 'Wat elke kind toekom'"* — false
   from the moment the title changed, which was a few hours later.
2. **A delivery order.** *"the writer delivers two or three possibilities and a person
   chooses"* — discharged the same afternoon.

When the choice came in I wrote the resolution into the *title note*, a neighbouring
field, and left the requirement still quoting the old title and still ordering the
delivery. A coverage check found it: a writer reading that field top-down would deliver
options for a settled title and might reopen it. It was the eighteenth field in that
sub-topic carrying a withdrawn order, and the first I had both created and let go stale
the same day.

**How to apply.** Two rules, and the second is the one I got wrong:

- A requirement states what must be **true of the lesson**, never what is **currently in
  it**. "The title may not claim X" survives any title; "the current title is Y" does not.
- When an order is carried out, **retire it in the field that gave it**. Recording the
  outcome in a neighbouring field does not reach the writer, because the writer reads the
  ordering field from the top. Point from the order to wherever the detail lives, rather
  than leaving the order live and the answer elsewhere. See
  [[die-korrigeerde-opdrag-bly-in-die-veld-staan]], [[die-merker-begrawe-die-bestelling]],
  [[n-spek-mag-nie-n-beslisde-bewoording-aanhaal-nie]] — all four are the same mistake
  wearing different clothes: **a spec field that repeats a fact instead of pointing at it.**

## A numeric breakdown: remove it, never re-proportion it

**1 October 2026, a sixth costume of the stale-order family — arithmetic rather
than enumeration.** A budget field stated the lesson's share as 440 words above
its own record marker, then itemised a rough plan summing to **470** — the figure
the field carried before two transfers out. The rewrite that fixed the headline
number left the itemisation carrying the old one. Live, so it told a writer the
budget bought thirty words that did not exist, against a hard envelope.

**The obvious repair is the wrong one.** Re-proportioning the breakdown against
the new total resets the clock on the same fault: it is a frozen state in an
ordering half, it had already gone stale once, and it goes stale again the next
time words move between lessons. **Move the breakdown below the record marker and
keep only the binding total**, which is what the gate measures anyway.

**And where the recorded arithmetic does not reconcile, say so rather than
mending it.** That field's record read 470 − 10 − 30, which is 430, not 440. I
could not establish which number was wrong, so the honest field states that the
recorded transfers do not reconcile and that the cluster division is
authoritative. See [[moenie-in-die-spek-skryf-wat-jy-nie-nagegaan-het-nie]] —
a guess written into a specification is read ever after as settled.

## Order the COVERAGE, not the position

**2 October 2026.** A writer found that the placement of a sentence was
load-bearing: a boundary in a glossary entry had to sit **after** both culpability
levels to cover both, and moved up it would cover only the intentional half. I was
about to have that ordered at source as a position — "it must stand after both".

A coverage check gave me the better form, and the reasoning generalises: **ordering
a position quotes a structure rather than stating a demand**, and it goes stale the
moment the field is legitimately reordered. Order the requirement instead — "the
boundary must cover both culpability levels" — which stays true under any
arrangement and which a writer can satisfy without copying a layout.

The same check named why it mattered: the field already ordered the reckless limb
and ordered the boundary, but never ordered that the boundary **reach** both. So a
writer could satisfy both orders separately, move the sentence, leave half the
definition uncovered, and coverage could not object — which is exactly how two
earlier rounds of that entry went wrong.

**The general rule: an order about arrangement is a frozen state. Restate it as an
order about what must be true.**

## The field I wrote yesterday is the one I forget to sweep

**9 October 2026.** On 8 October I suspended a faulty agreed wording and wrote a notice
into a specification field: *the injected list held this wording; it is suspended; a
writer who finds the term EMPTY in that list should deliver the form the core item
orders and report the sentence.*

On 9 October a person decided the term gets **no entry at all**. I swept that decision
across the core item, the expected-terms list and the shared-wordings note - and missed
the field holding my own notice. So a writer reading top-down was told that this lesson
carries the entry and instructed to deliver a wording, while the core item forbade one.
**My notice had frozen a state that lasted one day**, and the instruction fired exactly
when the state it described came true.

A coverage checker found it. Its observation is the useful part: the 9 October sweep
reached three fields and not this one, and the writer's own note said the previous
round's conflict was gone - because the writer had checked the core item and the
expected-terms list, which were the two I had fixed.

**Why I missed it.** I remember a field I wrote as *the fix*, not as *a carrier*. The
sweep I ran was a sweep for the old wording, and my notice did not contain the old
wording - it contained an instruction ABOUT the old wording, which is a different
string and the same hazard.

**How to apply.**

* **Sweep for the DECISION, not only for the wording.** After a ruling changes, the
  carriers include every field that describes the previous state - notices, cross-
  references, "what to do if" instructions - and none of them will match a search for
  the withdrawn text.
* **List the fields I touched in the previous round and re-read each one.** They are
  the likeliest carriers and the ones I will not think of. Keeping that list costs
  nothing; I already write it into every repair report.
* **Prefer a notice that states a rule to one that states a state.** "Do not write an
  entry for this term" survives a change of state. "If you find the list empty, deliver
  a wording" is a conditional on a state, and it detonates when the state arrives.

**12 October 2026 — I repaired a frozen-state note by writing another one, within the hour,
while repairing exactly this class of fault.** Two fields refused work on the grounds that
"the lesson is at its ceiling", which was false — there were fifty-one words spare. My fix
replaced that with "it measures 509 words against a ceiling of 560". A writer round landed
the same afternoon and the lesson became 519, so my correction was wrong before the day was
out, and a coverage check flagged it.

**A measurement is a frozen state, exactly like the claim it replaced.** The durable form
names no number at all: the prohibition rests on its own first reason, and whether there is
room is the gate's business and is read there, never asserted in a requirement. When
correcting a note that froze a state, check that the replacement is not itself a state — "it
measures N" and "it is at its limit" are the same mistake with different arithmetic.
