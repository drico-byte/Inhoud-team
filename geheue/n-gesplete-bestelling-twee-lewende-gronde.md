A fifth variant of the same week's fault, and the only one no sweep can catch. Correcting the
basis of one list item, I wrote a new sentence lower in the ordering half — the item rests on
how a house was BUILT and not on whether it had stone — and left the field's opening
enumeration saying the item is about how a home LOOKED. **Neither sentence was withdrawn.**
Both were live, both were defensible, and they order different things.

**Why it is worse than a stale order.** A stale order contradicts a record below it, so a
sweep that compares the two halves finds it, and a writer reading top-down meets a withdrawn
claim. A split order contradicts nothing: there is no record saying either ground was dropped,
the field reads as coherent, and the only symptom is that different readers obey different
sentences. By the time the coverage check found it, the draft had followed the opening line
and my own brief to that checker had stated the correction — the two readings were already
live in two heads.

**How to apply.** When correcting the BASIS of a requirement — what a claim rests on, what an
item is about, what the conclusion follows from — do not write the new ground as an addition.
Find the sentence that already states a ground and replace it, then read the whole ordering
half asking "how many grounds does this field now give for this item?" The answer must be one.
This is the same discipline as amending the opening line, with a sharper test: not "is the old
order gone" but "is there exactly one ground". Related:

[[n-regstelling-wat-n-opsie-bied-kies-die-volgende-fout]], [[kern-en-feiterisiko-weerspreek-mekaar]].

**By the end of 12 October 2026 the cross-field variant had happened SEVEN times**, and every
single one was found by a checker or a writer rather than by me. The shape: field A describes
field B — what B orders, what B carries, what state B's text is in — and then B changes. No
record withdraws A's sentence, because nothing about A was wrong when written, so no sweep
that compares an order against the record beneath it can see anything. The symptom is only
ever that two readers obey different sentences.

**The fix is not vigilance, it is a different habit: POINT, DO NOT DESCRIBE.** "The content of
the difference belongs to core item 4's list" survives any change to item 4. "Core item 4 lets
the list carry place and manner" does not. Same for a sibling's *field*: "that field holds the
delivered title and orders nothing" survives, while "that field's title breaks this ban" went
stale the hour I updated the title. Where a pointer genuinely will not do, the description has
to be re-read every time the field it describes is touched — which is a promise I have now
broken seven times in one week.
