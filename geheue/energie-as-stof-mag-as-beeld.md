Drico ruled on this after reading both sides. **Energy may be pictured as
something that moves and gets stored. What a lesson may never do is claim it IS a
liquid or IS a solid object being handed over.** As long as we stay on the simile
side of that line we are not lying, and the picture is how a nine-year-old gets
hold of the idea at all.

**Why:** energy really is conserved, so the stuff picture makes all the
bookkeeping come out right, and Scherr and colleagues built a whole teaching
approach on exactly that — children acting out a fixed amount moving between
objects. The opposing view, that the picture plants a misconception later grades
must undo, is a real position but not a settled finding. Our specification had
written the anti-metaphor side down as fact, which is the thing a specification
must never do, because nothing downstream re-checks it.

**How to apply:** an image for energy transfer is allowed and does not have to be
justified as an exception. Two constraints. First, the identity line: *moves like*,
*flows like*, *stored like* — never *is*. Second, and this is what the ruling does
NOT cover: [[the two heat analogies that failed earlier]] broke in two different
ways, and only one was an identity claim. Water made heat a liquid, which this
ruling now permits as a simile. But the ball analogy failed because it made the
cold object start with **nothing**, which is a mapping error and is still wrong.
So the picture is allowed; the mapping still has to be checked claim by claim.

Practical note that removes a false trade-off: the word budget measures study text
only, so an intuition layer costs nothing against a lesson's budget. There is never
a words-versus-image decision to make.
