8 September 2026, Gr 4 SW. The steam lessons had a mechanism error: they explained a
boiler's power by how **big** it was, and how much steam it held. That was corrected in
three lessons and in the standard's reference example, and the corrections were verified.

A narrow comparison check then found the same fault alive in a repaired block — wearing
different clothes. The block now said that being closed means the steam's push runs the
whole **length of the cylinder** instead of stopping at a pot lid. As the checker put it:
geometry is still doing the work that pressure should do, only it has moved from the
boiler's volume to the cylinder's length. And then the sentence that matters:

> Worth watching for, because it would pass a check that only greps for "big".

**Why the disguise is the danger.** Once a phrase is named as the error, that phrase is
what gets searched for — in the drafts, in the specs, in the reference example. The
underlying *wrong explanation* is what should be searched for, and it has many phrasings:
bigger, more, longer, further, for longer. Each one lets a learner reason to "more of the
same thing" when the truth is "a different amount of the same thing does not do it — the
push is harder, not bigger or longer".

The learner's destination is the tell, not the wording. In this case, both costumes end at
the same place: *engine steam is pot steam, just more of it.* A pot lid lifts at a
fraction of one pound per square inch; a pressure cooker reaches about fifteen; a
locomotive boiler runs at two hundred to two hundred and fifty. Nothing about size or
length closes that gap.

**How to apply.**

* **When you correct a mechanism, write down the false conclusion, not just the false
  words.** Then look for anything that reaches that conclusion by another route.
* **Do not close a mechanism finding on a keyword sweep.** A grep for the rejected phrase
  proves the phrase is gone, not that the explanation is fixed. The narrow comparison
  pre-check is what actually catches these, because it asks where a learner reasoning
  onward ends up — the same reason it earned its keep in
  [[elke-sin-waar-die-prentjie-vals]].
* **Suspect the repaired block hardest.** A writer fixing a named fault reaches for the
  nearest replacement, and the nearest replacement is often the same idea one step over.
  Two of the three steam blocks did exactly this.

**29 September 2026, Gr 4 Geography settlements: the third costume has no false
sentence in it at all.**

Lesson 6 claimed outright that services are harder to reach far from the big centres,
and extended that to electricity. A fact check killed it — rural household electricity
access in South Africa is 94.0% against urban 90.9%, and the real gap is formal versus
informal dwellings, which is a metro problem. Corrected at source, verified, closed.

One lesson later the same false picture was back, and **every sentence carrying it was
true**. Lesson 7 has four parallel place blocks — farm, village, town, city, smallest to
largest. The first three each answer food and water. The fourth answers food, and then
*electricity* and water. Nothing asserts that electricity arrives with size. The **shape
of the list** asserts it.

So the costume list now runs: a different phrasing (the boiler), a different object (the
cylinder), and **a different medium entirely** — position in a parallel structure.

**What this adds to how to apply.** A false conclusion can be delivered by arrangement,
and arrangement is invisible to every check that reads sentences. Two guards:

* When a claim is corrected, ask not only *where else is this said* but **where else is
  this implied by what sits next to what**. A parallel list is the obvious place: whatever
  appears in only one of its items is being attributed to what makes that item different.
* **Resist the caveat repair.** The checker here explicitly warned against fixing it by
  adding a qualifier to the city block, because a qualifier makes the ladder more visible
  rather than removing it. The repair for a structural claim is structural — either the
  odd item joins the others, or the thing it names appears elsewhere too.

cautions instead of the content), [[elke-sin-waar-die-prentjie-vals]],

**30 September 2026, Gr 6 SW Democracy lesson 4: the fourth costume is a different
quantifier — and the withdrawal note's own permission is the gap it came through.**

The title *Jou regte werk net as almal hulle deel doen* was withdrawn as false. My note
said which words had to fall — "NET" and "ALMAL" — and then added that the
responsibility half of the idea was sound and a new title might carry it.

The new title was *By elke reg staan 'n verantwoordelikheid*. Same universal, different
word, and my note had explicitly cleared the ground for it: it forbade saying rights
otherwise fail, and said nothing about asserting that a responsibility stands beside
**every** right. The lesson's own summarising sentence says "sommige ... ander".

**What this adds.** The costume can be the *quantifier*: net, almal, elke, altyd, enige,
niemand. They are interchangeable carriers of one claim, so naming two of them as the
fault reads as permission for the third.

And a new guard, about my own notes rather than the content:

* **A withdrawal note that grants a permission has to bound it.** "The idea is sound and
  a new title may carry it" is an invitation unless it says in what form. Write what the
  replacement may *not* claim, not only what the old one claimed — and when the fault was
  a universal, say that no universal in any word is allowed.
* **Count the withdrawals.** This was the second title lost from one lesson, and the
  second loss was caused by the record of the first. A second failure at the same spot
  means reading what I wrote there, not just what the writer wrote.

## Two more costumes, 9 October 2026: the substituted word, and the block heading

A read-only sweep of nine drafts against sixteen withdrawn claims turned up both.

**The substituted word.** The fault was calling the Cape of Good Hope Africa's southern
tip. The writers guarded it the obvious way and **left the Cape's name out of every
lesson** - one lesson's note records that as a decision. The claim came back anyway, in
the other direction: three lessons now put **at the southern tip** the things that
actually happened **at the Cape**. Rounding it unseen in the weather and sighting it on
the way home, in one lesson; the refreshment stop for fresh water and food, in two more.
With the substitution, every one of those sentences became a claim about Cape Agulhas,
and all of them are wrong - and the one that says they "wel gesien" it is worse than the
original, because it makes a specific identified sighting out of it.

**So removing the name does not remove the claim. It relocates it into whatever word
replaced the name**, and the replacement is the word nobody is watching. When a ruling
forbids a term, sweep the SUBSTITUTE as hard as the term.

**The block heading.** One lesson's prose was built sentence by sentence to avoid CAPS's
claim that a southern African society peaked during the Renaissance - "al lank" is doing
exactly that work, and the note records that CAPS's "gedurende dieselfde tyd" was struck
on purpose. **The block's heading is "Suider-Afrika in hierdie tyd"**, which asserts the
contemporaneity the three sentences underneath it were written to avoid, over a block
that names a kingdom two centuries older than the window the lesson has just fixed.

Nothing in the provenance note shows anyone looked at the heading. The guard held in the
sentences and failed one line above them. Same family as
[[elke-les-kry-n-eie-naam]], where nothing checks a title the way it checks the text -
and a BLOCK heading is even less watched than a lesson title, because it is short, it
reads as navigation rather than as content, and a sweep written for sentences skips it.

**How to apply.** Add both to the repair checklist: after a ruling removes a word, grep
the replacement; and read every heading in the lesson against the claim being withdrawn,
not only the prose. Also worth knowing that a heading CAN reach a fact checker - one
caught a shape claim in a heading in the same batch - but it will not reach a sweep I
write for requirement fields, and it did not reach the writer's own review.
