Lesson 24's specification named the thing the lesson must *resolve*: that in
everyday Afrikaans a person says **"sit dit hoër"** at a radio and means *make it
louder*, so a nine-year-old arrives already reading *hoog* as *hard*. It was
written into two fields, one of them labelled "the most important word entry in
this specification", and a 54-word block was built to confront and correct the
phrase.

**Afrikaans says "harder". "Sit die radio harder."** Drico settled it in one
line. English really does say "turn it up higher", and the specification had
imported that problem wholesale — most likely because the planner reasoned about
Afrikaans from general knowledge, which is exactly the ground the fact checker's
own prompt says it will be confident and wrong on.

**What survived and what did not.** The *conceptual* confusion is real and
language-independent — children do conflate loud with high — so the lesson still
has to resolve it, and it already did, in the cross-test, without any claim about
how people talk. What went is the block that taught learners an expression in
order to correct it, which is worse than never mentioning it. What also changed
is the protected-word rule's standing: everyday Afrikaans already agrees with the
lesson's own words, so the risk was overstated. The rule stays because it costs
nothing and a writer thinking in English can still reach for *hoër*.

**How to apply:** a claim about how Afrikaans speakers actually talk is not a
requirement until Drico or the outside language checker has seen it. The fact
checker cannot settle one — it correctly refused this one and marked it unsettled
for two rounds rather than deciding on overseas sources, which is its own
recorded rule working. Ask early: this one held a finished lesson at the last
step, and it had been sitting in the spec since planning. Related:
[[moenie-in-die-spek-skryf-wat-jy-nie-nagegaan-het-nie]] and
[[afrikaans-wag-vir-spesialiste]].

**It happened a second time — 29 September 2026, Grade 7 LO, Health 5 — and the
resolution generalises.** The block on what to do when you are worried said what
*verklik* MEANS ("to get someone into trouble"), and the whole distinction between
fetching help and telling on someone hung off that sentence. Every source the fact
checker could reach defines the word by the **act** (to inform on, to betray), not
by the purpose; he refused to rule from Dutch and English, correctly, and marked it
unsettled.

**The fix was not to settle the word. It was to stop the block needing it.** The
teaching is right and language-independent: fetching help for someone is not telling
on them, and the difference lies in what the child is *trying to do*. That stands
with no definition at all. So the sentence saying what the word means comes out, and
there is nothing left to settle.

**The rule that makes this decidable without an Afrikaans speaker:** the outside
language check runs AFTER the fact check, so **no claim in a lesson may depend on its
answer.** When a block rests on what a word means, the question is not "what does the
word mean" but "does the block need the claim at all" — and it usually does not,
because the teaching sits under the word rather than in it. Only if the claim is load
bearing does it become a question for Lampies.

Both times the shape was identical: a *conceptual* point that is real, wrapped in a
*usage* assertion that was never checked. Keep the point, drop the assertion.
