**8 October 2026.** A fact check of the explorers specification reported that our account
of the 1488 landing at Mossel Bay omitted a successful barter the sources lead with, and
quoted the phrase "the sailors were able to buy by barter cattle and sheep". I put that
in a repair brief as a correction to make, in my own words, as established.

**It is backwards.** The repair agent read the derived sources in full and found: there
was no barter in 1488 - with no interpreter the two sides could not speak and the people
drove their cattle inland; the *Roteiro* explains a later reception by saying that when
Dias was there the local people "fled without taking any of the objects which he offered
them"; and the site's own museum says plainly that no bartering took place. The quoted
phrase is verbatim from a widely-read secondary page and looks like **Da Gama's visit to
the same bay ten years later, shifted back** - garbled even there. **And the
stone-throwing I had been told was thinly sourced is in the same sentence as the killing**,
so it is better attested than the barter, not worse.

Had the agent obeyed me, it would have written a false claim into an approved
specification **with my authority behind it**, where it would have read as settled.

**The thing that makes this sharp: I guarded the right way in the same brief, once.** On
the walling claim I wrote "verify Van Waarden (2011) yourself before acting, and if what
you find differs from this description, report that rather than acting on my version."
That one held and the agent confirmed it. On Mossel Bay I simply asserted. **The claim I
guarded was the claim that was true; the claim I did not guard was the one that was
backwards.** I had no way of knowing which was which, which is the whole argument for
guarding both.

Three smaller errors travelled in the same brief, all from the same cause - repeating a
checker's summary in my own voice: Great Zimbabwe's peak is the 14th and 15th centuries,
not the 13th and 14th; the coast Dias sailed beyond his predecessor's furthest point is
about 350 leagues, over 1 600 km, and the 1 400 km I gave is a different distance
entirely; and for Jupiter's moons the contested point is whether another observer worked
independently, not the date.

**How to apply.**

* **A finding is evidence; an instruction is an order.** The moment I restate a checker's
  finding as something to fix, the next agent will do it. So every relayed claim gets the
  same sentence: *this came from another check, verify it against a source before acting,
  and report back if it differs.* Not only the ones I happen to doubt.
* **Attribute, do not absorb.** Say "the checker reported X, citing Y" rather than "X is
  the case". The agent then knows what kind of thing it is holding.
* **A widely-repeated secondary page is where a shifted or garbled detail lives**, and
  two events at one place a decade apart is exactly the shape that gets collapsed. When a
  claim is about one visit to a place that was visited more than once, check which visit.
* **Welcome the refusal.** Two agents today declined parts of my briefs - this one, and a
  writer that refused credit for work it had not done. Both were right, and both said so
  plainly rather than quietly doing it my way. That is the behaviour to keep asking for,
  and it only works if the brief says disagreement is wanted.

## A writer refused a cut I ordered, and showed me the requirement ordered it twice

**9 October 2026.** A fact check could not fasten down one clause - that ordinary people
spoke to the king through others - and I told a writer to drop it, adding: *check before
you cut, and report rather than comply if a requirement demands the mechanism; my
reading is that it asks only for restricted access.*

**It refused, and it was right.** The mechanism is ordered **twice**: once in the core
item verbatim, with a semicolon separating the seclusion from the mechanism as a second
demanded thing, and again in a neighbouring item that restates it as a standing order
while narrowing something else. Coverage tests against those items, so cutting would
have failed the corrected draft on a requirement stated in two places, and the next
revision would have put it back and been able to cite the field.

**Two separate errors of mine, and the second is the one to fix.**

* I acted on the newest of three checks without weighing the others. Two earlier ones
  had confirmed the clause, one of them singling it out as the mechanism that most
  easily goes wrong - that is, as something the lesson had got RIGHT.
* **I told the writer what the requirement asks for without reading the requirement.**
  "My reading is that it asks only for restricted access" came from my memory of a
  coverage report, not from the field. That is the same failure as relaying a checker's
  finding in my own voice, applied to our own specification, where I have no excuse at
  all - the file is right there.

**What saved it was one sentence in the brief.** "Report rather than comply if it goes
the other way" is why the error did not ship. **Put that sentence in every brief that
orders a cut**, and mean it - an agent that is told disagreement is wanted will spend
the effort to check, and an agent that is told to cut will cut.

**And the right disposal for the clause is acceptance, not removal.** It rests on the
site's principal authority's reconstruction of the royal court; two checks confirmed it;
no source states it for commoners; nothing contradicts it. That is precisely an
`onseker` item of the kind a dated acceptance record exists for - see
[[bronne-weerspreek-mekaar-dek-ons-met-een-sin]]. Cutting a supported reconstruction
because the third check wanted a source for the exact population would have been the
more destructive answer, not the more careful one.

**9 October 2026 — a brief needs the same evidentiary standard as a spec edit, because that
is what it becomes.** Twice in one afternoon I handed a repair agent a premise about the
drafts that I had not checked, and both times the premise would have been written into the
specification as settled fact if the agent had simply obeyed.

First: I said a technical term survived only in a lesson's TITLE, so the ban I ordered covered
titles only. The term is used twice in that lesson's third block, so the contradiction I was
repairing stayed alive in the text. The agent verified the narrower, true version of my claim
— that no requirement of that lesson orders the word — and wrote the field on that instead,
then told me.

Second: I gave the reason for a correction as "the region's earlier farmers already did all
of this before the period". The conclusion was right but the reason was wrong: that valley had
no farmers at all before the period opened, and the whole package arrived with them at the
start. The correct argument runs from the earliest centre at the opening of the span, and it
is STRONGER than the one I gave.

**How to apply:** before a premise about a draft or about the evidence goes into a brief,
check it the way you would check a sentence you were about to write into a requirement — or
mark it explicitly as a hypothesis to test and invite refutation. The invitation is what saved
both of these. Related: [[moenie-in-die-spek-skryf-wat-jy-nie-nagegaan-het-nie]],
[[my-soektogte-mis-en-dan-glo-ek-hulle]], [[n-voorbehoud-kan-die-verkeerde-risiko-dek]].

## 13 October 2026: true is not the same as permitted, and I told a writer to keep a withdrawn sentence

Gr 6 SW Medicine lesson 6. Briefing a writer to replace the withdrawn germ clause, I added a
guard of my own: *there is a separate sentence about the germ being too pale to recognise even
under a microscope — that one is true, and it must stay exactly as it is.*

It **is** true. It had also been **withdrawn at source**, in capitals, in the very core item
the writer was reading: the obstacle was not faintness, and *"pale things must be dyed"* is a
general inference a learner carries to every germ, where it is false. The real obstacle is
this bacterium's thick waxy coat repelling an ordinary dye. The writer obeyed me, flagged the
contradiction between the draft and its own requirement, and was right on both counts.

**What I actually did:** I had seen that sentence in an earlier scan, checked it for *truth*,
found it true, and filed it as safe. Then I promoted "true" to "must stay" in a brief, which
is an order. I never asked the only question that mattered — does a requirement forbid it?

**The distinction to keep.** A sentence can fail on at least three independent tests: it can
be **false**; it can be **true but withdrawn**, because the reasoning it teaches generalises
wrongly; and it can be **true and permitted but unrequested**. Checking one tells you nothing
about the others. A fact check answers the first. Only the specification answers the second.

**How to apply.** Never write "this must stay" or "leave this as it is" into a brief about a
sentence I have not looked up in the requirement that governs it. If I want to protect
something, the honest form is "I believe this is fine — check it against the field before
keeping it", which costs the writer nothing and leaves the judgement where the evidence is.
And a guard I add to a brief is not a smaller thing than the fix I asked for: it arrives in
the same register and gets obeyed the same way.
