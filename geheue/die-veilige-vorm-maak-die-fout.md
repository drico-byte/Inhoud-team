**8 October 2026, the Graad 6 explorers specification.** A fact check on the document
found eleven false claims. **Several of them were not in the requirements. They were in
the guards** - the fact-risk items whose whole job is to keep a writer out of trouble.

The sharpest one is a loop. The file forbids "ontdek", for the right reason: it writes
the voyage from one side only. The form it offers instead is *"hy was die eerste
Europese seevaarder wat ... "*. **That is a precedence claim**, and precedence claims in
this subject are usually false or contested:

* "the first European seafarer to arrive in India by sea" - Greek and Roman seafarers
  did, from the 1st century BCE. The dropped qualifier is **around Africa**.
* "the first to see and record these things through a telescope" - Thomas Harriot drew
  the Moon through one on 26 July 1609, months before Galileo. He never published,
  which is the only reason the credit went elsewhere.

And the file's own writing note says, in terms, that superlatives are the highest-risk
category in a history lesson and that **none of them is needed here**. Three survived
anyway, two of them false - and **all three arrived as the safe replacement for the
forbidden word**. A writer is told to treat them as the honest form, so nobody questions
them.

**Why this is worse than an ordinary false claim.** "Ontdek" is a framing fault a reader
NOTICES - it reads as a point of view, and a checker can object to it. "The first
European to" reads as a neutral fact, so it passes every eye it meets. The repair traded
a visible fault for an invisible one.

**How to apply.**

* **A prohibition without a prescribed replacement is half a rule.** When I forbid a
  form, I have to say what to write instead - and then test the replacement against the
  fault class I most fear. Here: reached, sailed to, arrived at, or "the first we have a
  record of" would all have done the work without claiming priority.
* **Fact-check the guards, not only the requirements.** A guard is written as the honest
  form, which is exactly why a false one travels furthest. In this file the guards held
  the wrong tip of Africa (permitted as a fact), a prohibition on citrus against scurvy
  that history contradicts, and two of the three bad superlatives.
* **A guard can contradict the lesson it guards.** The scurvy prohibition contradicted
  four other ordering items that rest on the fresh-food link - and the prohibition was
  the thing that was wrong. See [[kern-en-feiterisiko-weerspreek-mekaar]]: kern wins in
  practice, but that is about which one REACHES the draft, not which one is right.
* **Check which sentence you are adjudicating.** The same file tested a CAPS
  simultaneity claim about Great Zimbabwe against Mapungubwe's dates, citing Mapungubwe
  sources throughout - two different CAPS sentences on two different pages, merged. The
  ruling may survive; its stated reason does not, and four fields carry the reason.

**A field can demonstrate the ambiguity it forbids.** 14 October 2026, Medisyne lesson 7. One kern item's opening example sentence fused a wound and an illness into one clause, and two lines later the same field said "make sure the subordinate clause does not read onto the wound as well". The example *was* the forbidden form. A writer who copies the example obeys the field and breaks the rule in the same stroke, and the prohibition is then evidence that the form was intended. Same field, same day: a "want"-join welded a separate time-limitation order onto the end of the wound order, so the limitation read as the reason for the wound rule rather than as its own requirement. **When a field forbids a shape, read every example and every joined clause in that field for the shape itself.**
