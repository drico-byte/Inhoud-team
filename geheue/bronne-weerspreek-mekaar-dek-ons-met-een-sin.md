**Lampies, 1 October 2026**, on a Grade 6 history lesson. Five source families put
Fatima Meer's first banning in 1952 after the Defiance Campaign — the strongest of
them matching our three restrictions word for word. Two put it in 1954, and one of
those two is her own sworn testimony to the Truth Commission, where she corrects
the record outright and gives a different cause. Her biographer agrees with her.
One archive records *both* orders, and its own two pages disagree on the duration.

His ruling: *"keep 1952 and the campaign and very briefly add that she herself
howver sayd it was banned in 1954 and for other reasons (dont give the reasons). It
should just be to cover ourselves. So 1 short and sweet sentence."*

**Why this is worth keeping:** it is a third option I had not offered. I gave him
cut-it, keep-the-majority, or follow-her-testimony — and my own standing rule
([[onsekere-ekstras-word-gesnoei]]) would have cut. Cutting was wrong here because
the year and the cause are a real anchor in a biography, and picking a side was
wrong because both sides are strong. The answer is to **print the better-supported
reading and disclose the conflict in one sentence**.

**How to apply.** The sentence is a *hedge, not a discussion*: it says she herself
gave a different year and different reasons, and it does **not** give the reasons,
weigh the sources, or name who said what. One sentence. The writer words it.

This does not replace the cut-it rule — that still governs an extra nobody needs
and a word choice that cannot be settled. It governs the narrower case: a fact the
lesson genuinely needs, where sources conflict and one side is the subject's own
account of their own life. Related: [[die-ooreengekome-bewoording-kan-self-verkeerd-wees]],
[[drie-maniere-om-n-bewering-reg-te-maak]], [[onseker-kan-ongetoets-beteken]] — and
note this was a real conflict with sources reached on both sides, not an untested
claim, which is why cutting was not automatic.
