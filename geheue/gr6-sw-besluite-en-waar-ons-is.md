## Mapungubwe: fact-check the requirements before the lessons

**The practice that changed everything here, 7 October 2026.** Before any of its ten
lessons entered checking, I had the **specification itself** fact-checked as a document.
Roughly 170 assertions, **11 contradicted or materially wrong**, 7 of them live orders,
and **two already sitting in drafts**. None could have been found by the per-lesson
pipeline, because the fact checker never sees a requirement and a draft matches its
requirement perfectly. **Do this for every remaining sub-topic.** See
[[spesifikasies-word-nooit-nagegaan]].

That file also had **no record marker anywhere**, so every dated correction sat inside a
live ordering field. 23 markers were introduced, and three orders had to be lifted out of
records first — filing a record that issues an order below the line withdraws a
requirement by tidying.

**The rulings:**

* **Mapungubwe is a *koninkryk*, not a *staat*.** My decision on Lampies' delegation, 7
  October. The requirement had ordered "first state" unhedged and forbidden every hedge,
  on two reasons that were both false — the heritage bodies say "first indigenous
  **kingdom**", and the dissenting camp declines "state" for everything, so it concedes
  nothing. CAPS's own topic title says kingdom. **We decline the typology dispute rather
  than taking a side**, and no lesson tells a learner about it.
* **The claim is bounded and the bound is inside the sentence** — the first kingdom that
  *arose here in southern Africa itself*. Dropping the clause makes a false absolute, so
  the clause is protected, not just the word.
* **`koninkryk` gets no agreed definition.** Defining it against `staat` re-enters the
  dispute; `staat` is the technical word and is defined, this one is ordinary Afrikaans.
  Define the hard word, not the easy one — recorded for reconciliation with the hazard
  named, so the silence reads as a decision.
* **`staat` keeps its glossary entry in lesson 2**, because CAPS's cluster heading uses
  the word. It is not a softening of the kingdom claim.

**The trap peculiar to this sub-topic:** the reading copy's header prints CAPS's own
`kaps_punt` on **every** PDF, so the language checker reads *"eerste staat"* and
*"Eerste dorp"* directly above a text that says neither. Every protection here has to say
that the header is a citation of the curriculum and not the lesson's claim — see
[[die-leeskopie-het-ons-interne-notas-uitgestuur]].

## The year's rulings

* **No article number of the Constitution in a learner's text**, anywhere in the
  sub-topic. CAPS asks for none at this grade. Bound at subject level, not per
  lesson. Citations in a spec field are provenance and never travel.
* **`reg` belongs to a person's entitlement and is never used in the law sense**
  in this subject-grade. That one ruling moved four agreed wordings in a day.
* **Grade 6 says `gelyk voor die wet`; Grade 7 says `voor die reg`.** Each grade
  keeps its own rule. The protected-words list still needs grade scoping for this —
  until then one entry forbids what the other requires.
* **A municipal by-law stays `wet`, not `verordening`.** Declined **eight** times
  now. A fact checker never sees a spec, so this cannot stop recurring; the refusal
  is recorded so the ninth raising lands as answered.
* **The limitation rule drops its scope qualifier.** Lampies, 1 October: section
  36(1)'s "law of general application" cannot be stated truly at this grade, so the
  element is **cut, not approximated**. An independent fact check later confirmed
  the omission makes the stated protection narrower than the real one, so the lesson
  asserts nothing false.
* **Fatima Meer's first banning stays 1952 with the Defiance Campaign**, plus one
  short sentence that she herself said 1954 for another reason. Lampies, 2 October,
  re-affirmed after new evidence that the two records may be one order logged
  twice. Her reasons are not given; the sentence exists to cover us.
* **The four names of harm keep their four-way split** — our teaching convention,
  approved knowing no source carries four distinguishable meanings.

  **This ruling has now been through three reversals. Read all of it before
  touching those four entries.**
  (a) "Every entry is narrower than its source word" is **gone**: the wider entry
  carries the statute's own genus, so it logically contains the narrower one, and
  the sources nest the two rather than setting them side by side. **The demand that each entry name something the other three do not is
  ITSELF withdrawn, 7 October 2026** — see (d). What remains is that each
  entry be true, stand alone, and not carry another's definition as its own.
  **Do not re-order strict separation**, in either costume. (b) The deliberateness element binds the **wider** entry only, where the
  Act's own definition states it. It never did the work claimed for it: **a
  dentist acts deliberately**, and what excludes a dentist is the word *harm*.
  **The recklessness limb this part once ordered is GONE — see (d).**

  **(c) The medical boundary that (b) once named is GONE — cut by Lampies on
  4 October 2026, both sentences including the injection-and-dentist
  illustration — and no exception may be written in its place, in any shape.**
  Five rounds of repair all failed the same way, and the fault was never where
  the repair went: the entry's **opening clause** made plain hurting sufficient,
  so every round had to carve legitimate hurt back out, and every carve-out was
  exploitable. One hung on *purpose*, reproducing reasoning the Constitutional
  Court struck down in 2019 — an adult passes that test out of his own account.
  The next hung on *who acts*, which shielded a whole class and would have told
  a child harmed by a nurse that her case did not count.

  **The sixth round fixed the opening clause instead, and then no exception was
  needed.** The hurt must now be wrongly given or cruel, carried by an ordinary
  adjective on the **treatment** — not on the actor, the purpose or the place,
  all three of which have already failed. An injection is then not a child being
  treated wrongly, so it never reaches the entry at all. What keeps it from
  becoming a defence is that it is only the *opening*: the next sentence says
  hitting and kicking count, with no qualifier, so an adult who hits is reached
  directly and never gets as far as arguing about his treatment.

  **When a definition needs an exception, suspect the definition.** The real cost
  of the over-wide opening was never the medical direction — it was that it is
  the clause an adult inverts to discredit the sentence that matters, which is
  *once is enough*.

  **(d) THE FOUR-WAY SPLIT IS NOT A SPLIT. My decision, 7 October 2026, on
  Lampies' delegation, after primary-source legal research.** A fact check found
  *misbruik* already covered everything *mishandeling* described, both ending in
  the same sentence, with only the unverifiable recklessness clause between them.
  The research settled why that could not be rewritten away:

  * The four terms **are the Constitution's own** — *mishandeling, verwaarlosing,
    misbruik of vernedering*, nouns, "of" not "en". **Section 240 makes the
    English text prevail.** So all four names stay; dropping one means not
    teaching a word the clause uses.
  * **Only *abuse* and *neglect* are defined anywhere** (Children's Act 38 of
    2005 s 1). *Maltreatment* and *degradation* are defined nowhere — nor are
    *harm* and *ill-treatment*, the two words the abuse definition rests on.
  * **Assault and deliberate injury sit expressly inside *abuse*** (s 1 "abuse"
    para (a)) — the opposite of where we had put them.
  * **No authority distinguishes maltreatment from abuse.** The Act pairs the
    words in eight provisions and never contrasts them; *FORSA* [2019] ZACC 34
    para 68 writes "assault proper, maltreatment or child abuse" as three labels
    for one thing; CRC General Comment 13 says they carry equal weight. **The
    overlap is in the sources, not in our wording** — so no rewrite could fix it.

  **Two findings corrected my own brief, and both cut against the recklessness
  limb:** *neglect* has **no mental element** ("a failure in the exercise of
  parental responsibilities"), so a careless carer is already caught — and that
  singular "a failure" is what now *supports* **one failure is enough**. And
  **UNCRC article 19 is limited to harm suffered in the care of a carer**, so the
  provision cited for the limb never reached the careless stranger it was cited
  for. Under s 39(1)(b) a court must *consider* international law; s 231(4) means
  it cannot *supply a definition*.

  **So: keep the four names, stop manufacturing a distinction.** *Misbruik* takes
  the hitting and deliberate injury the Act puts there; *verwaarlosing* keeps
  one-failure-is-enough and sheds any seriousness qualifier as part of the
  *definition*; *vernedering* keeps dignity harm without physical injury
  (*Teddy Bear Clinic* [2013] ZACC 35 para 55, decided on s 10, not s 28(1)(d));
  *mishandeling* is allowed to be the broad word it is. **Overlapping true
  definitions beat four tidy false ones**, and a glossary entry only has to be
  true and stand alone — not to be exclusive.

  **What this honestly leaves uncovered, and the lesson must not pretend
  otherwise:** a child harmed carelessly by someone who is **not** a carer falls
  between the two defined words — *abuse* needs intent but not a carer,
  *neglect* needs a carer but not intent — and no word of the four reaches him.

  **The Afrikaans trap, which is what a future reader will fall into.** The
  Children's Act is gazetted in **English only**, so there is no Afrikaans
  statutory definition of any of the four; and the Domestic Violence Amendment
  Act 14 of 2021's official Afrikaans renders **abuse as *mishandeling*** — the
  very word the Afrikaans Constitution uses for maltreatment. **Never rest a
  distinction on the Afrikaans words.**

  **Not reached, which is not the same as uncertain:** Supreme Court of Appeal and
  High Court authority (those repositories sit behind bot detection, correctly not
  bypassed), so "no South African court distinguishes them" is established only
  for the Constitutional Court; and the law-reform report that would say whether
  omitting a definition of *maltreatment* was deliberate. See
  [[onseker-kan-ongetoets-beteken]].

## The sign-off gap — DECIDED 7 October 2026

Sign-off demands two clean verdicts and has **no human-accept path**. But the
standing rule for conflicting sources is to keep the best-supported reading and
disclose the conflict in one sentence — which escalates honestly on **every**
future fact check. Lesson 5 is exactly there: coverage clean, text ruled correct,
held by one unresolvable fact. **Lampies approved it on 7 October 2026**, together with a second fix, and
both are being built:

* **A dated acceptance record** names the one unsettlable item, the date, who
  accepted it and why. Sign-off proceeds only when every item holding the verdict
  up is individually accepted; one unaccepted item still refuses, and a blanket
  acceptance is forbidden by design. An acceptance lapses if the item's text
  changes, so it cannot become a hole later content falls into.
* **Sign-off compares the draft's CONTENT, not the file's clock.** The old test
  asked whether each report was newer than the draft *file*, using the clock as a
  stand-in for "has the text moved since it was checked" — and the stand-in is
  wrong, because writing the status field or the working note moves the clock
  without changing a word any checker reads. It produced a false refusal on a
  finished lesson 8 whose fingerprint was provably unchanged. It now compares
  against **the last recorded content change**, and separately asserts the draft's
  current fingerprint still matches that record.

**One hole stays open and is NOT approved:** sign-off still cannot tell a finished
check from an abandoned one — see
[[n-feitetoets-op-kinderskade-word-afgesny]]. Removing the clock test may widen it,
since the clock was the only thing that would catch a truncated report.

## Only two fields in a lesson are free to edit

`status` and `herkoms.nota` sit outside the runner's freshness hash. **Everything
else — including the spell-checker's word list, which reaches no learner and
cannot change a verdict — makes the runner archive and delete both check reports.**
A one-word tidy can cost a 35-item web-heavy fact re-check. See
[[notaherstel-bly-in-die-nota-veld]].

## What this subject costs, and why

About twenty runner rounds per lesson. Life Skills ran at half the rate: it
teaches behaviour, where a fact check has little to bite on, while this teaches
dates, named people and constitutional mechanics.

**The dominant failure, twenty-three instances in one sub-topic:** a requirement's
instruction left standing after a correction below it withdrew it — in six
costumes, including a breakdown that no longer sums to its stated total, and a
correction spliced mid-sentence that leaves the order unreadable as prose. Several
were mine. **Three of the four faults in one field were my own patches**, each
fixing the sentence a check had named and leaving the rest of the paragraph
describing the world before the ruling. See
[[die-korrigeerde-opdrag-bly-in-die-veld-staan]]. The reliable detector is an agent
reading the field from the top; three attempts to mechanise it failed and are
recorded in the sweep itself.

**And five instances of the same fault inside a draft's working note**, which
nothing in the pipeline reads against our rulings —
[[die-herkoms-nota-kan-self-n-bestelling-word]].

**Eight coverage checks across five lessons found the draft correct every time.**
Not one fault was in a lesson a child reads. The writers have been right; the
instructions have been wrong.

**Before the next sub-topic enters checking:** settle its shared wordings first,
**fact-check its requirements before anything is written**, and read every working
note against our rulings before sign-off. One false claim of mine in a requirement
cost about ten agent runs; checking that sentence first would have cost one. See
[[spesifikasies-word-nooit-nagegaan]].
