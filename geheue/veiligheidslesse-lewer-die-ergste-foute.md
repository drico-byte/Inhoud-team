Gr 5 Life Skills, 19 Sep 2026. The worst findings of the whole year's run were in the safety lessons:

- Poison in the eyes or on the skin was given the same instruction as swallowing. It must be rinse immediately, 10-15 minutes, THEN get help. Delay costs eyesight.
- The paraffin story said paraffin "looks nothing like cool drink". It is clear and colourless like water, which is exactly why it is the commonest childhood poisoning in South Africa. The story also had the child open and smell the bottle (the vapour is itself a poisoning route) and carry it open through the house.
- The lesson told children an adult may never mix a rehydration drink at home. False — official guidance shows caregivers how.

**Why:** in every other lesson a wrong sentence teaches something untrue; here it tells a child what to DO.
**How to apply:** for any lesson carrying an instruction, brief the fact checker on the instructions specifically, and read each one as if a child follows it exactly. Lampies' ruling on the recipe: the lesson prints no numbers AND never says an adult may not mix it — if the clinic is far, an adult asks there what to mix.

## A carve-out's TEST matters more than its scope — never make it a purpose

**2 October 2026, and I caused it.** To narrow a maltreatment definition I told a
writer to "exclude legitimate hurt without excluding reckless harm". That invites
a carve-out, and the writer wrote a careful one: medical help a child needs is not
maltreatment even though it hurts, **because both are there to help the child.**

A fact check found it hands a hurtful adult a defence:

* It was **unconditional** — not tied to a health worker, not to treatment
  lawfully consented to — and whether the hurt was "needed" was left to the
  judgement of whoever was doing it.
* Its reason was a **purpose test**, which any adult can apply to his own conduct.
  **That is the exact reasoning the Constitutional Court struck down in 2019** when
  it removed the defence of chastisement "for the child's own good".
* It therefore **contradicted the sentence immediately before it** (hitting and
  kicking count), and which one a reader obeys depends on the reader.

**How to apply.** When a definition of harm carves out a benign case, tie the
exception to **who does it and in what setting**, never to **why**. And ask
whether an eleven-year-old needs the exception at all: the cost of a sentence an
adult can lean on is higher than the cost of her briefly wondering about a
dentist.

## The test that found it, which belongs in every safety fact brief

No finding asked for this; I added it on a hunch and it was the only thing that
caught the defence:

> **Could any sentence in this set be read by an adult as a justification for
> hurting a child?** Where an entry carves out something that is not harm, check
> whether the carve-out can be read wider than it is written.

Pair it with the other direction — **could a harmed child conclude her case does
not count?** — and state both as stated negatives, because an untested test looks
exactly like a clean one.

## And a closed list implies something no source says

The same check found the deeper cause of a coverage gap: **every source gives an
OPEN list with a catch-all** ("and any other physical, emotional or moral harm or
hazards"), while the lesson gave **four closed boxes**. The gap was not a
mis-drawn boundary; it was implied exhaustiveness. A glossary set that divides a
field needs to say it is not the whole field — the same job `onder meer` does in a
list block. See [[kaps-se-lys-wen-oor-die-handboek-se-lys]].

## When a definition needs an exception, suspect the DEFINITION

**4 October 2026, after five rounds on one glossary entry.** The entry defined
maltreatment as *hurting or treating a child cruelly*. Because plain hurting is
not wrong in itself, that forced a carve-out for legitimate hurt — and every
carve-out we wrote was exploitable: a **purpose** test the adult passes out of his
own account (the reasoning the Constitutional Court removed in 2019), then a
**person** test that shielded a whole class, so a child harmed by a nurse could
read that her case did not count. Lampies cut the exception; a fact check then
found the real fault.

**The opening clause was the fault all along.** It stated a *sufficient* condition
that is not sufficient. In both languages, and in the Act's own phrasing — "harm
**or ill-treatment** deliberately inflicted" — the wrongfulness lives in the word
itself. Put it in the definition and **no exception is needed at all**; the
deliberate-injury, recklessness and once-is-enough clauses all survive inside the
narrower claim.

**How to apply.** When a definition seems to need an exception carved out of it,
do not design the exception. Ask what the defined word already means and whether
the definition has dropped it. Four rounds went into patching the carve-out and
none into reading the first clause.

**And the cost of the too-wide clause ran the opposite way to the obvious one.**
It was never really that a child would think an injection was maltreatment — it is
that an over-wide opening is *the clause an adult inverts to discredit the sentence
that matters*, that once is enough.
