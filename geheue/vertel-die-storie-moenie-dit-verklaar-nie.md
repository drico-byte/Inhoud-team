Drico, 2026-08-25, planning the Grade 4 Term 4 lesson on Moon stories: "The story
should not be dissected and explained. Just tell the story, and if needed, make a VERY
BRIEF 'what's the point of the story' at the end of it."

## The ruling

A folk tale that gets analysed for a nine-year-old stops working as a story. So the
lesson **tells** each story and stops. At most one short line at the end on what it
points to — and only if the story does not already carry it.

I had suggested the opposite: stories plus framing that draws out what they have in
common. That was wrong for this content. CAPS's own bullet is that cultural stories
"tell us about the importance of the Moon in people's lives", and a story about why the
Moon changes shape **is** that evidence. It does not need a paragraph explaining that it
is evidence.

**About 100 words a story is fine**, and four stories at that length is a reasonable
lesson. That is an approximation, not a cap — Drico was explicit about this.

## For the Moon stories lesson specifically

The Grade 4 textbook names five peoples' Moon stories: **Xhosa, Sotho, Tswana, Venda and
San.** That list came from a keyword count over the book's own pages, not from reading
its retellings, so no textbook expression entered the project.

Drico's position, and it is right: the stories are traditional and belong to the
cultures that tell them, not to whoever printed them last. What stays the publisher's is
only their particular retelling. So we tell the same stories, sourced independently, and
the fact checker verifies each attribution against sources outside the textbook — the
same discipline as lesson 17's house names.

**Expect the story count to be decided by what is verifiable, not by the budget.** If
two source cleanly and three do not, the lesson is two stories. Cut rather than pad:
[[onsekere-ekstras-word-gesnoei]].
