Drico, 10 September 2026, in his own words: **"we NEVER keep a false claim."** He
agreed the framework below in the same conversation, after asking whether we were
over-correcting into vocabulary a Grade 5 learner does not have.

When a claim is too wide or false, there are three ways to fix it:

1. **Narrow the quantifier.** "Every" to "most", "all" to "many". Costs no new
   vocabulary at all. This is what nearly every fix turns out to be.
2. **Cut the claim.** Say less. Costs words, not vocabulary.
3. **Teach the mechanism.** Costs new vocabulary, and at Grade 5 is usually the
   wrong answer.

**Why:** his worry was real — if a learner does not know the word used to describe
something, the description is pointless, however accurate. But the conflict arises
far less often than it looks, because (1) and (2) are almost always available.
The danger of accepting his framing too readily is that "they won't know the word"
becomes a licence to keep a false claim when a free fix existed.

**How to apply:** reach for 1, then 2. Choose 3 only when the other two genuinely
cannot carry it, and say so explicitly. A writer refusing to introduce a term
because the grade's depth limit forbids it is doing this right — one refused
"stroom" for electricity on exactly these grounds, arguing that an honest vague
sentence beats a sharp false one. Related: [[onsekere-ekstras-word-gesnoei]],
[[n-versagting-vat-die-algemene-helfte-saam]].

## A fourth, for a number: drop the quantity, keep its function

**9 October 2026.** A lesson said archaeologists know of more than a hundred settlements
in the valley from before Mapungubwe. One fact check named a survey giving 104 for the
two earlier phases, so I ruled the figure in. **The next check could not reach any
pre-Mapungubwe count at all**, and the only "more than a hundred" it found belongs to
the Mapungubwe period itself - so my ruling had moved the span rather than fixed it. It
also raised two things neither of us had: archaeological *sites* are not *settlements*,
because rock shelters and rain-control sites are counted in; and a count across three
centuries is cumulative, not a snapshot of how many stood at once - which is exactly the
reading the next sentence invited.

**I had shipped a number on one check's citation.** Two checks then disagreed about which
period it covered, which is footing I should not decide a quantity on.

**The repair is cheap, and it is a fourth option worth having beside narrow, cut and
teach the mechanism: drop the QUANTITY and keep its FUNCTION.** In that sentence the
number was doing no work - what the sentence had to do was give "two of them" something
to be two of, and make "the rest" the small settlements, which is the only thing
licensing the comparison that follows. All of that survives without a count.

**How to apply.**

* **A count is almost never load-bearing; its role in the sentence usually is.** Before
  defending a figure, ask what the sentence needs it FOR. If the answer is a comparison
  or a contrast, the figure can go and the comparison stays.
* **Never decide a quantity on one source's authority**, and treat a disagreement about
  which period or scope a figure covers as fatal to the figure rather than as a detail.
  A number is the most quotable thing in a lesson and the hardest to hedge.
* **Watch the neighbouring sentence.** A cumulative count beside a "two of them" turns
  itself into a snapshot without either sentence saying so.
