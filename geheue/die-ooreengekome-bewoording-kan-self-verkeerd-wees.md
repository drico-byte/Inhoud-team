Grade 4 Life Orientation, 2 September 2026. Two terms drifted and writers
decided one wording each. Both decisions were sound. Then the fact check found
one of the agreed wordings **factually wrong**: a temple was defined as a
building where people *gather* to worship, and gathering is precisely what
defines a church, a mosque and a synagogue rather than a temple — visiting a
Hindu temple is not a congregational experience, and daily Hindu worship happens
at a home shrine.

So the term went through three wordings. The house rule says a third wording
makes it worse than leaving it alone. That rule is about drift, and it has to be
overridden when the fact is the problem: **a wrong agreed wording is worse than
a changed one**, and it is worse than drift, because drift is at least visible
to the sweep.

**Why:** reconciliation asks "do these agree?" and a fact check asks "is this
true?". Passing the first says nothing about the second, and once a wording is
in the agreed-wordings file it is read as settled by every later lesson.

**How to apply:** the process already says to fact-check shared definitions as
part of drafting the year — do it **after** reconciling, not before, or you
check a wording that is about to be replaced. And when the wording changes,
sweep the prose that leaned on it: five sentences moved with this one, and
leaving them would have set two lessons against each other. See
[[n-regstelling-ontwrig-sy-bure]] and [[n-skoon-toets-is-so-wyd-soos-sy-omvang]].

## It can be wrong twice, and the obvious repair can be the wrong half

8 September 2026. `ketel` was settled three times in one day, and the first two were
both wrong.

1. **"Die groot houer…"** — reconciled from two drifting lessons. A narrow comparison
   check found that size is not what makes a boiler work, and that the entry read
   alone described the covered pot in the lesson's own analogy.
2. **"Die toe houer…"** — the writer's proposal, and it looked obviously right:
   closedness is exactly what the pot lacks. Two independent checks then found it
   also fails. In Afrikaans *toe* includes *bedek*, and *ketel* already means a
   closed vessel for heating water, so the sentence still described a lidded pot and
   a kitchen kettle.
3. **"Die sterk houer waarin water gekook word om stoom onder hoë druk te maak."** —
   approved by the same checker that rejected the first two. **Pressure** is the
   discriminator: a pot vents at the rim and a kettle at the spout, so neither makes
   steam under high pressure.

**The lesson is about which half you reach for.** Closedness is *necessary* and it is
what the counter-example lacks, which is exactly why it feels like the fix. But
necessary is not distinguishing. Ask what the definition must *exclude*, then check
the candidate word against those objects one at a time — in the language the lesson
is written in, because *toe* carried a sense its English gloss does not.

**And the third wording was chosen by leaving something out.** The single controlled
outlet is the other real discriminator, and the writer argued it out of the sentence:
a kettle's spout reads as a pipe to a nine-year-old, closing that would mean naming
the engine (describing what the boiler is *attached to* rather than what it *is*),
and a one-sentence entry cannot carry a chain — fire, boiler, pipes, engine needs the
block. **The block carries the chain; the entry carries the property.** That division
is the reusable part.

Two things worth keeping from how it ended. The residual was *recorded rather than
discovered*: read alone the wording also fits a pressure cooker and an autoclave, and
that is acceptable because a child picturing a pressure cooker has the **right**
physics, where a child picturing a lidded pot had physics that cannot work at all. A
residual that teaches true mechanism is a different kind of thing from one that
teaches a false one. And the wording keeps the rump of both earlier attempts, so it
reads as the third pass at one sentence rather than a fourth different sentence —
which matters, because a definition may not get richer as the year goes on.
