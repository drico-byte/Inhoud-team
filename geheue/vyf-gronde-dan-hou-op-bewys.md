Gr 6 SW Mapungubwe lesson 4, 13-14 October 2026. One block had to support the conclusion
that gold objects were symbols of royal power. I ordered five different grounds over two
days and a fact check refuted every one:

1. **only the king's people had gold** — sources carry rank→gold, not the converse;
2. **they wore it visibly** — all the evidence is funerary, and the social use of these goods
   cannot be proven;
3. **found on the hill and almost nowhere else** — the negative half has no positive source,
   and the find-place turned out to be the same find as the burial, so the set was circular;
4. **the stone walls show where the leaders lived** — no source says that, the hill carries
   little stone work, and the literature is divided on what the walls prove;
5. **the bodies were buried upright and seated** — the museum reading says so, but the
   specialist who worked through the 1930s excavation archive says the seated position
   cannot be clearly established, no articulated bones were seen, one grave may be a
   reburial, and **the grave the rhino itself came from has no posture record at all**. The
   step from posture to rank is an ethnographic comparison from elsewhere, not an
   observation at the site.

**The sixth ground was not the answer.** The archaeology is contested at every link, and a
230-word Grade 6 lesson cannot carry that proof at all. What is solidly sourced is the
conclusion as the *mainstream reading* — which is also what CAPS asks the lesson to teach.

**So the block stopped proving and started attributing.** One thing stated as fact (most of
the gold we have comes from a few graves on top of the hill), and everything after it
expressly what researchers infer. That is honest, it costs fewer words than the chain it
replaced, and it ends the cycle because the block no longer defends anything.

**The rule to carry: count the grounds, not the rounds.** Three rounds on one *wording* means
the form is wrong ([[n-bewoording-wat-drie-rondes-vat-is-die-verkeerde-vorm]]). But three or
more distinct *grounds*, each refuted on its own evidence, means something different — the
claim is contested in the literature and the lesson is trying to settle it. At that point:

* stop looking for a better support, and ask whether the lesson needs to prove the thing at
  all;
* if the conclusion is the mainstream reading and the curriculum asks for it, **attribute it**
  — "researchers conclude" is true, cheap, and teaches a real thing about how we know;
* and check that the premises are not stated flatly while only the conclusion is hedged. That
  asymmetry was present in every one of the five versions and no sweep looks for it.
