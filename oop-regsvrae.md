Prepared 28 September 2026, for Grade 7 material on what a child should do when

## Question 1 — does the reporting duty bind a child?

### First, a correction to the question's premise

**There is no longer a section 54(1)(a).** The whole of section 54 was substituted by
section 26 of Act 13 of 2021, in operation **31 July 2022**. The duty is now section
54(1); the offence of failing to report is section 54(2)(a).

The **old** wording (in force 16 December 2007 – 30 July 2022), quoted verbatim by the
Constitutional Court at para 18 of ZACC 35:

> "(1) (a) A person who has knowledge that a sexual offence has been committed
> against a child must report such knowledge immediately to a police official.
> (b) A person who fails to report such knowledge as contemplated in paragraph (a),
> is guilty of an offence and is liable on conviction to a fine or to imprisonment for
> a period not exceeding five years or to both a fine and such imprisonment."

The **current** wording, section 54(1):

> "A person who has knowledge, reasonable belief or suspicion that a sexual offence
> has been committed against a person who is vulnerable as defined in section 40 must
> report such knowledge, reasonable belief or suspicion immediately to a police
> official."

Two substantive changes, not just renumbering:

1. The victim class is now "a person who is vulnerable as defined in section 40".
   Section 40 defines `'person who is vulnerable'` as meaning a — "(a) child or a
   person with a mental disability; (b) female under the age of 25 years who …"
   (and three further categories). **Children are still covered.**
2. The trigger was lowered. It used to take **knowledge** for a child victim; it now
   takes "knowledge, **reasonable belief or suspicion**". A suspicion is now enough.

### Finding A — the text: "a person", no age carve-out

**Yes, and this is firm.** The duty-bearer is "A person". The Act does not define
"person" at all, and there is no age qualification anywhere on the duty-bearer, in
section 54 or in the section 1 definitions. On the literal wording a child is included.

The only age definition that touches section 54 is the definition of the **victim**:
section 1, `'child'` means "a person under the age of 18 years" (substituted by section
1 of Act 5 of 2015, wef 7 July 2015).

One historical trap, now closed: before 7 July 2015 the section 1 definition of "child"
had a second limb — "(b) with reference to sections 15 and 16, a person 12 years or
older but under the age of 16 years". Act 5 of 2015 deleted it. It was a narrowing of
the **victim** class for two sections only, and it never applied to section 54.

### Finding B — has anyone held that it applies in practice to children?

**No. I found nothing.** This is a separate finding from Finding A, and it is the honest
answer: the point is **not settled**.

- **The Constitutional Court did not decide it.** *Teddy Bear Clinic* was an
  application confined to sections 15 and 16 (paras 2–3). The applicants and the first
  amicus both attacked section 54(1)(a) in argument (paras 28, 34), and the Court
  described the duty at para 60 as one falling on "trusted third parties" who "are
  obliged by section 54 … to disclose information which may have been shared with them
  in the strictest confidence, on pain of prosecution". But the Court's order sets aside
  the High Court order and replaces it with five paragraphs that **make no order about
  section 54 at all**. Section 54 has never been declared invalid, in whole or in part.
- **Every reported prosecution I found was of an adult.** In *K.G.S v S* [2026]
  ZAGPPHC 793 the accused's wife was convicted on two counts under section 54(1)(a) and
  sentenced to five years. *S v A.M* [2024] ZAECELLC 31 and *Kave v National Minister of
  Police* [2024] ZAECELLC 27 likewise concern adults. I found no reported case of a
  child charged under section 54, and no judgment that discusses whether a child is
  bound by it.
- **A practical brake, which is not the same as an exemption.** Under the Child Justice
  Act 75 of 2008 as amended by the Child Justice Amendment Act 28 of 2019 (in operation
  19 August 2022), the minimum age of criminal capacity is **12**: a child below it may
  not be arrested, charged or prosecuted at all; and for a child 12 or older but under
  14 the presumption is that criminal capacity is absent, and the State must rebut it
  beyond reasonable doubt. A Grade 7 learner — typically 12 or 13 — therefore could not
  realistically be convicted of failing to report. **But that is about whether a child
  can be punished, not about whether the duty exists.** (I took this from the
  Department's own media statement of 25 August 2022, not from the amended section 7
  text itself.)

### Answer to Question 1

The statute says "a person" with no age carve-out, so on its face it binds a child. No
court and no official guidance has held that it applies in practice to a child, none has
held that it does not, and I found no prosecution of a child under it. Treat it as
**unsettled**.

## Question 2 — consensual sexual activity between two children

### What the law now says

Sections 15 and 16 were **substituted in full** by sections 2 and 3 of Act 5 of 2015.
Both now read the same way, section 15 for sexual penetration and section 16 for sexual
violation. Section 15(1):

> "A person ('A') who commits an act of sexual penetration with a child ('B') who is
> 12 years of age or older but under the age of 16 years is, despite the consent of B
> to the commission of such an act, guilty of the offence of having committed an act of
> consensual sexual penetration with a child, **unless A, at the time of the alleged
> commission of such an act, was — (a) 12 years of age or older but under the age of 16
> years; or (b) either 16 or 17 years of age and the age difference between A and B was
> not more than two years.**"

Section 16(1) is identical, substituting "sexual violation".

So, as the law now stands:

- **The age band is 12 to under 16.** If both children fall inside it, there is no
  offence — including at the extremes of the band (a 12-year-old and a 15-year-old are
  both within paragraph (a)).
- **The line with an age gap.** If the older person is **16 or 17** and the younger is
  12–15, there is no offence provided the gap is **not more than two years**. If the gap
  is more than two years, it **is** an offence. Section 15(2)(a) and 16(2)(a) then
  require the relevant Director of Public Prosecutions to authorise the prosecution in
  writing.
- **18 and over: no exemption at all.** An adult who does this with a 12–15-year-old
  commits the offence.
- **Under 12: different offence entirely.** Sections 15 and 16 do not apply below 12.
  Section 57(1): "Notwithstanding anything to the contrary in any law contained, a male
  or female person under the age of 12 years is incapable of consenting to a sexual
  act." Such conduct is rape or sexual assault, not a "consensual acts" offence.
- The age of consent remains 16. Act 5 of 2015's own preamble records that the objective
  of protecting children aged 12–15 "from adult sexual predators remains unaffected …
  and consequently also does not lower the age of consent in respect of sexual acts to
  12 years."
- One defence, narrowed: section 56(2)(a) still allows A to contend that the child
  deceived A into believing she or he was 16 or older and A reasonably so believed —
  unless A is related to the child within the prohibited degrees (section 56(3)). Section
  56(2)(b) was deleted by section 9 of Act 5 of 2015.

### The reporting duty — the part that matters most

**No reporting duty was removed by the amendment.** I checked this against the amending
Act's own text rather than against any summary. Act 5 of 2015 has fourteen sections. It
amends sections 1, 46, 47, 48, 50, 51, 56 and 67 of the principal Act, substitutes
sections 15 and 16, inserts section 69A, and amends the long title and the index. **It
does not touch section 54.**

The duty nevertheless does not bite on conduct that falls inside the exemptions, and it
does so by a different route. Section 54(1) is triggered only where "a sexual offence
**has been committed**". Where the conduct falls within section 15(1)(a)/(b) or
16(1)(a)/(b), no sexual offence has been committed, so there is nothing to trigger the
duty.

That is an inference from the text, and it is the reading taken in South African
academic commentary — the *South African Medical Journal* (2016) states that "The
mandatory obligations regarding the reporting of any sexual offence against a child
remain in place. Section 54 of the Sexual Offences Act has not been amended", while
reporting is required only where the conduct is still an offence (one party under 12,
non-consensual, a gap of more than two years with a 16- or 17-year-old, or an adult
partner). **I found no judgment and no official departmental directive confirming it.**
Mark it as a well-founded reading of the statute, not a judicially settled one.

Three cautions that follow from the text:

1. **Do not cite the Constitutional Court's moratorium as the current source.** Order
   para 3 of ZACC 35 placed a moratorium on investigations, arrests, prosecutions and
   ancillary proceedings against children under 16 in relation to sections 15 and 16,
   "pending Parliament's correction of the defects in the Act", and para 111 explained
   that "This moratorium will put in abeyance any related reporting obligations which
   may otherwise have arisen from the operation of section 54 of the Act." That was
   **interim relief**, expressly tied to the 18-month suspension, and it expired when
   Parliament corrected the Act. The current position rests on the conduct no longer
   being an offence, not on the moratorium.
2. **Since 31 July 2022 suspicion is enough.** An adult who suspects that the ages or
   the consent are such that an offence *was* committed is under the duty on that
   suspicion alone.
3. **Where the gap exceeds two years, the duty applies immediately.** It is not
   conditional on the Director of Public Prosecutions deciding to authorise a
   prosecution; those are separate provisions.

### Answer to Question 2

Two children both aged 12 to 15 commit no offence, whatever the gap inside that band. A
16- or 17-year-old with a 12–15-year-old commits no offence if the gap is two years or
less. Beyond that, and with any adult, it is an offence. There is **no reporting duty
attached to consensual activity that falls inside those exemptions** — but not because a
duty was repealed. Section 54 was never amended for this purpose; the duty simply has
nothing to attach to once the conduct is not an offence. That last step is my reading of
the statute, shared by South African academic commentary, and **not confirmed by any
court or official directive I could find**.

## Where general summaries disagreed with the South African sources

Reported because the disagreements are instructive, and in each case the South African
primary text wins.

1. **"The 2015 amendment changed section 54."** Web summaries told me that "Clause 18
   (amending section 54) aims to make the reporting duty the same for children and
   persons who were mentally disabled". That was a clause in a *Bill*, and not in the
   Bill that became Act 5 of 2015. The enacted Act 5 of 2015 contains no section 54
   amendment. The equalisation actually happened through Act 13 of 2021, wef 31 July
   2022 — seven years later.
2. **The commencement date of Act 5 of 2015.** Several secondary sources, including an
   SAMJ article, say 3 July 2015. The Act contains **no commencement clause** (section
   14 is a short title and nothing more), it was assented to on 7 July 2015 and
   published in *Government Gazette* No. 38977 of 7 July 2015, and the consolidated text
   records the section 15 and 16 substitutions as "wef 7 July 2015". Use **7 July 2015**.
3. **"Failing to report is a felony."** A summary used American terminology. South
   African law has no felonies; it is a statutory offence carrying a fine or up to five
   years' imprisonment, or both.
4. **Section numbering.** Several current-looking summaries still cite "section
   54(1)(a)" as live law. It was renumbered out of existence in 2022. Note that a
   *judgment* may legitimately still cite 54(1)(a) where the offence predates the
   substitution — *K.G.S v S* was handed down in July 2026 about conduct charged in 2017.

## One adjacent point, verified, because it bears on the lesson

A different statute governs professional reporting, and it is **not** framed as "any
person". Children's Act 38 of 2005, section 110(1), lists a closed set of occupations —
including **teacher**, nurse, medical practitioner, social worker, psychologist,
religious leader and traditional leader — who, on reasonable grounds concluding that a
child has been physically abused, sexually abused or deliberately neglected, **must**
report. Section 110(2): "Any person who on reasonable grounds believes that a child is
in need of care and protection **may** report that belief …". A child is not in the
section 110(1) list, and section 110(2) is permissive, not compulsory. Section 110(3)(b)
protects a good-faith reporter from civil action.

So: a teacher who is told is under a duty under that Act in her own right. That is a
firmer thing to build a lesson on than the child's own position.

## What a Grade 7 lesson may safely assert

### On reporting

The lesson may say, plainly and without hedging, that the law expects *anyone* who
knows or suspects that a child has been sexually abused to tell the police straight
away, and that the law does not say "only grown-ups". It may say that a learner who
tells a trusted adult, a teacher, or the police is doing exactly what the law wants, and
that a teacher who is told is required by law to take it further — so telling a teacher
is not a dead end. It may say that someone who reports in good faith cannot be sued for
it.

The lesson may **not** say that a child who stays silent is committing a crime, or could
be arrested, charged or punished for staying silent. Nobody has ever decided that, no
child has been prosecuted for it as far as I can establish, and a learner of this age
could almost certainly not be convicted in any event. Equally, the lesson may not say
the opposite — that children are excused — because nothing says that either. The safe
shape is: the expectation to speak up rests on everyone, and the *responsibility to act*
rests on the adults who are told. Never use fear of punishment as the reason to report;
it is not established law, and it is the wrong reason.

### On consensual activity between children

The lesson may say that two children of about the same age who choose to do something
sexual together are not criminals, that the law used to treat them as criminals, and
that it was changed for exactly that reason. It may say, without qualification, that an
adult who does this with a child is committing a serious crime, always, whatever the
child said or agreed to. It may say that a very young child cannot agree to anything
sexual at all, and that agreement from such a child counts for nothing in law.

The lesson may **not** say that it is "allowed" or "legal" in any general sense. The age
at which the law says a person can agree to sex is unchanged, and saying otherwise would
be wrong. It may not give a learner an age rule to apply for herself: once one of the
two is older, a gap of a few years crosses the line, and the lesson cannot teach a
thirteen-year-old to work out where that line falls. And it may not tell learners that a
nurse, doctor or counsellor is obliged to report a same-age couple to the police — that
is not so, and printing it would stop children asking for help. Because the fine detail
here is unsettled and some of it turns on facts a child cannot judge, the safest content
is the part that is certain: nobody may touch you without your agreement; an adult who
does it is committing a crime; and asking a nurse, a doctor or a counsellor for help is
not confessing to one. Do not make promises in the lesson about what any particular
professional will then do.
