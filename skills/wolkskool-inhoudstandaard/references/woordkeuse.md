# Word choice — the house list

Prefer the everyday word. **If a word is not one a nine-year-old would use in
conversation, either replace it with one they would, or give it a `begrip` entry.**

## Why this list exists, and why nothing else can do its job

The gate measures how *long* a word is — letters and syllables — and never whether
it is *known*. `oewer` is five letters and two syllables. `ranke` is five letters and
two syllables. Both pass every threshold in the register bands with room to spare.
The coverage checker looks at completeness, the fact checker at truth. **So a lesson
can be entirely correct, comfortably inside its band, and still full of words a
learner has to guess at**, and nothing in the pipeline will say so.

Four such words reached the human review of the very first lesson. This list is
where each one gets fixed once instead of recurring for four hundred lessons.

It is the one check no script can perform, because it needs a native speaker's
judgement about what a nine-year-old actually has. It grows by one line every time a
word is flagged in review — so read it before writing, and add to it when you are
told about a word.

## Avoid / use instead

| Avoid | Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `oewer` | `die ander kant`, `die kant van die rivier` | Correct, but not a word a Grade 4 has. The plain phrase is also easier to picture. |
| `ranke` | `tou` | Introduced while correcting a factual error and traded one problem for another. Plant-fibre rope is what sources describe, and `tou` says it accurately in a word every child owns. |
| `peddel` | `spaan` | `spaan` is both the familiar word and the correct one. Afrikaans sources use the bare word for exactly the canoe implement, and define a `spaan` as one handled *entirely free of the boat* — which is what a canoe uses. `peddel` is a loanword that buys nothing. |
| `opstoot` | `dryfvermoë`, `dryfkrag` — or plain verbs | `opstoot` is a coinage, not standard Afrikaans, and it reached a glossary entry in the first lesson before anyone checked. The Afrikaans sources carry the force in plain verbs — *die water stoot op* — which is also how a nine-year-old would say it. If the concept must be named, `dryfvermoë` is the word. |
| `heet` (= is called) | `ons noem dit`, `dit staan bekend as` — or just name the thing | Correct Afrikaans, and that is the trap. `heet` as a verb means *is called*, but the word a nine-year-old owns is the adjective `heet` = **hot**. So "blare aan heet 'n steggie" offers a temperature as its first available reading. Drico, a native speaker, stopped on it and asked whether it was a typo for `het` — if it caught him, it will bury a learner. Passes every automated check: correct, short, one syllable. |
| `bene` (an animal's legs) | `pote` | Drico's rule: for a **dier** we always write `pote`. `bene` means both *legs* and *bones*, so in a lesson that also says a backbone is made of small bones, using it for legs makes the same word do two jobs a page apart. `pote` can only mean an animal's limbs. Keep `bene` only where the subject really is bone, and there `beentjies` is usually the natural word. **CAPS itself gets this wrong** at Grade 5, writing "sommige het geen bene" with worms, millipedes, insects and spiders as the examples — which would collide head-on with "an insect has six legs". Do not inherit it. |
| `roeiriem` | `spaan` (for a canoe) | A `roeiriem` is the implement fixed to the boat in a rowlock, for rowing. That one genuinely is wrong for a canoe. |

**A correction worth keeping, because it nearly went the other way.** The
English oar/paddle distinction does *not* map onto Afrikaans, and assuming it does
led the orchestrator to record that `roeispaan` meant a fixed oar and that `spaan`
was a familiar approximation of the precise word. Both were wrong: in Afrikaans the
fixed implement is the `roeiriem`, and `spaan` is the precise term. The vocabulary
choice happened to survive the bad reasoning. **Do not reason about Afrikaans
vocabulary from English cognates** — check it, the way any other claim gets checked.

## Words that look like padding and are load-bearing

Some hedges carry the truth of a sentence and a simplification pass must not strip
them.

- **`meestal`** in "hierdie eenvoudige bote het meestal op riviere en mere gevaar".
  Without it the sentence is false: there is evidence of plant-built seagoing craft
  tens of thousands of years old. The hedge is what makes the claim survive.
- **`droë`** before both the wood and the reeds. Soaked or green wood can be denser
  than water and sink; the word is what makes the floating claim true.
- **`van klei`** after `baksteen`. A bare brick could be a cement brick, and cement
  comes from limestone, which formed from sea creatures' shells. The two words are what
  keep the object on the clean side of the origin test.
- **`self`** in "hy word nooit self groter nie". Fired bricks do permanently expand after
  the kiln, about half a millimetre to a millimetre per metre, by taking up moisture from
  the air — which is why walls need movement joints. The claim is true because the brick
  adds nothing of its own. Drop `self` and it becomes false.
- Any softened superlative — `een van die eerste`, `het gehelp om te vestig`,
  `het gewys dat`. These were softened *because* the absolute form was checked and
  found false. Restoring the crisp version restores the error.

If a word looks like it could be cut for plainness, check whether a fact checker put
it there.

## Settled phrasings — do not "correct" these

- **`al sewe die lewensprosesse`** is right. The writer flagged it as reading oddly and
  suggested `al sewe lewensprosesse` or `al die sewe lewensprosesse`; Drico, a native
  speaker, reads the original as perfect. It appears in both Grade 4 living-things
  lessons. Leave it.

The general point: **an agent's sense of what sounds natural in Afrikaans is not
reliable evidence, in either direction.** It has been wrong about a word being wrong,
and elsewhere wrong about a word being right. Where naturalness is the only question,
it is a native speaker's call and takes them a second — ask rather than reason.

## Two failures that look alike and are not

**An unfamiliar word** is fixed by swapping it. That is what the table above is for.

**A dense sentence** is not a vocabulary problem at all, and swapping words will not
touch it. "Te veel vrag maak die vlot swaarder as al die water wat hy kan wegstoot"
has short plain words and a low syllable count, and the gate rated it as good as
anything in the lesson. It is hard because it asks a learner to hold two abstract
quantities at once and compare them.

**If a sentence needs the reader to hold two abstract things at once, split it or
make it concrete.** The plain version — "As die vrag te swaar word, kan die water die
vlot nie meer hou nie" — is the same length and says the same thing in the language
the block already uses. The precise formulation does not belong in the lesson at
all — it arrives cold in study text, and the `eli10` layer that used to take it was
abolished on 23 September 2026. Say the plain version, or leave the precision to the video.

## When a source has its own authoritative Afrikaans

The Constitution is published in Afrikaans as well as English, so for legal content
there is an authoritative Afrikaans wording for everything a lesson says — and it is
often not the natural Grade 6 Afrikaans. Every occurrence is a fork, and the fork was
taken four times in one day, each time from scratch, before this was written.
**Lampies' rule, 1 October 2026.** It covers any source with its own official
Afrikaans text, not only the Constitution.

Work out which branch you are in before choosing the word. These are not a preference
order; they are a test, and the first question is always whether the claim moves.

**1. If the difference changes the CLAIM, use the source's word**, even where it reads
worse.

Section 28(1)(c) gives a child `basiese voeding`. `kos` is not a plainer way of saying
that, it is a different claim: `voeding` is the minimum quantity and kind a child's
development needs, and `kos` is satisfied by enough bread. The Constitution uses two
words deliberately — an adult gets access to `voldoende voedsel` under section
27(1)(b), a child gets `basiese voeding` — and that pair is the evidence that the word
was chosen rather than merely used. This one was handled as a register choice for a
day and cost a round: the article 28 list took the correction while the prose block
beside it kept `kos`, and a coverage check had to find it.

**2. If it changes only the REGISTER, use natural Afrikaans.**

Section 35(3) says `billike verhoor`; the lesson says `regverdige verhoor`. No claim
moves — the right described exists and is described correctly — and `regverdig` is
already this grade's fairness word, with `regverdige handel` and `onregverdige handel`
defined over in the Handel sub-topic.

**3. If the source's word collides with a word this subject has RESERVED, use the
natural one and record the collision.**

Section 9(1) says `gelyk voor die reg`; Grade 6 says `gelyk voor die wet`, because
`reg` in this subject-grade belongs to a person's entitlement (Lampies, 1 October
2026), and the source form would put two senses of one word inside one sentence — the
same sentence already carries `die reg op gelyke beskerming`.

**This branch is decided per grade, not once for the subject.** Grade 7 goes the other
way on the same phrase, because its specification requires every right worded as the
Constitution words it. The two protected entries contradict each other on purpose and
may not be reconciled: whoever finds them side by side chooses the grade, not the
wording.

**A NAME is not a claim.** What a municipal council makes stays `wet` rather than
`verordening`. The Constitution's own Afrikaans calls it a `verordening` (section
156(2)), but section 151(2) does vest the legislative authority in the council, so the
substance is right and only the label differs. Checkers raise this one again and
again, each of them correct about the fact and none of them reporting anything new. A
label is not free, though: broadening `wet` to cover a council's rule made a later
block's correct narrowing invisible to a learner, and the repair belonged in the study
text rather than in the word. After taking this branch, check what the broader label
flattened.

**4. If the source's precision cannot be carried at this grade, CUT the element —
never approximate it.**

Section 36(1) requires an `algemeen geldende regsvoorskrif`. The lesson had rendered
that as a rule that `vir 'n hele groep geld`, which lets a rule aimed at a single
group pass the test — the very thing the clause exists to prevent. Lampies ruled on
1 October 2026 to drop the qualifier. What remains is true and simpler, and it drops
the test rather than mis-stating it. An approximation of a legal standard teaches the
wrong standard, and nothing in the wording tells a learner that it has been loosened.

**And the part that makes the rule work: record which branch was taken, every time, in
`kaps/beskermde-woorde.json`.** The outside Afrikaans language check will reverse any
divergence from the source it discovers, in good conscience and correctly by its own
lights, and that file is the only thing that reaches it. A branch-2 or branch-3
decision that is not there is silently undone, and nothing re-checks a lesson after
that pass. **Give the reason and not just the prohibition** — a checker who
understands why holds the line when a sentence reads awkwardly.
